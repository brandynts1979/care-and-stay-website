From the Founder

Why We Created the Stay Culture Journal

What makes people want to stay? It is a deceptively simple question, yet its implications reach into nearly every aspect of organizational life. We recruit, hire, promote and pursue growth. But attraction is only the beginning.

A Stay Culture is not simply a retention strategy. It is a philosophy of leadership, organizational development, care and sustainable business growth. It begins with appreciation for what is best in people, curiosity about what is possible and a commitment to creating that future together.

Through this Journal we will explore four outcomes: quality of life, quality of care, quality of work and quality of growth. Our purpose is not merely to provide answers, but to inspire better questions. Welcome to the Stay Culture Journal. — Brandyn Simmons, Founder, Care & Stay

Cover feature

People Are the Company

Organizations often describe people as their greatest asset. The intention is generous, but the metaphor is incomplete. An asset belongs to an organization; a person brings knowledge, judgment, relationships and aspirations that no organization owns. People do not merely support the company. Through their daily decisions and interactions, they constitute it.

Consider what a customer experiences when an employee has the authority to resolve a concern, or what a family experiences when a caregiver has enough time and support to listen. Those encounters are the organization made visible. A mission statement cannot compensate for a daily experience that contradicts it.

An appreciative approach begins by discovering where the organization already lives its values. Ask people to describe a moment when they felt proud of their work. Listen for the conditions that made that moment possible: a trusted colleague, sufficient resources, meaningful autonomy or a leader who listened. Then ask how those conditions might become more common. This is not an invitation to ignore unsafe conditions, inequity or poor performance. It is an invitation to address them while building on real capabilities.

Care & Stay identifies seven connected conditions: purpose and belonging, trust and safety, appreciation, growth, voice and influence, sustainable work, and leadership credibility. These are not seven slogans to put on a wall. They are questions about the experience of work. Does a person have a credible way to raise concerns? Can they learn without humiliation? Are workloads compatible with a sustainable life? Does leadership follow through?

Start with three conversations this week. Ask a frontline employee about a time their work mattered, ask a supervisor what enables excellent work, and ask a departing or long-serving colleague what would make staying worthwhile. Identify one recurring condition, choose a practical change with the people affected, and return in thirty days to ask what improved and what did not.

The aim is not to persuade everyone to stay at any cost. People have legitimate reasons to leave. The aim is to build an organization that deserves their commitment while respecting their freedom to choose.

Leadership feature · QSR & franchise

Promotion Is Not Preparation

A strong crew member may be promoted because they are dependable, fast and excellent with customers. A strong clinician may become a supervisor because of clinical expertise. Both deserve recognition. Neither promotion, by itself, teaches the skills required to lead.

The transition changes the definition of excellent work. An individual contributor succeeds largely through personal performance; a leader must create conditions in which other people can perform. Taking over every difficult task can make a shift run smoothly today while preventing the team from developing tomorrow.

Imagine a new restaurant shift leader watching a teammate struggle during a rush. Rather than criticizing the teammate or permanently taking over, the leader can stabilize the immediate situation, then debrief privately: What worked? What got in your way? What would help you handle this next time? The same pattern works in healthcare and education when adapted to safety requirements and professional responsibilities.

Prepare emerging leaders before promotion through observation, guided practice and feedback. Invite them to facilitate a brief huddle, delegate a manageable task, practice a difficult conversation and reflect on how their behavior affected others. Pair each activity with an experienced mentor who can model expectations and explain the reasoning behind them.

A practical thirty-day transition plan can include a first week of role clarity and listening, a second week of delegation practice, a third week of coaching conversations, and a fourth week of feedback and reflection. Evaluate observable behaviors, not just operational output. Did the manager set clear expectations, invite input, protect safety and follow through?

Promotion should be a beginning, not a graduation. Organizations that invest in preparation give emerging managers a fair opportunity to succeed and their teams a better experience of leadership.

Healthcare insight

Beyond Retention

Retention is an important measure, but it is not the whole purpose of a healthy care organization. Caregivers need predictable expectations, respect, a meaningful voice and conditions that allow them to provide safe, compassionate care.

Ask a team about a recent shift when care felt especially good. What made that possible? Then ask what repeatedly prevents that experience. Address concrete barriers such as unclear handoffs, inadequate resources or unsustainable workloads. Do not substitute appreciation for staffing, pay or safety improvements.

Choose one improvement with frontline staff and review its effect on both their experience of work and the quality of care. A culture worth staying for is created with caregivers, not merely for them.

Education insight

Caring for Those Who Educate

The experience of students is closely connected to the conditions in which educators work. Teachers need opportunities to collaborate, exercise professional judgment and receive support when demands exceed capacity.

Invite educators to describe a time when they felt able to do their best teaching. Identify the resources, relationships and leadership behaviors that contributed. Protect time for those conditions, and remove unnecessary demands where possible.

A useful leadership question is not only how we can improve instruction, but what we can change about the system so educators can sustain excellent work.

Appreciative business development

Building Possibilities

A sales conversation can begin with more than a search for pain points. It can begin with curiosity about the strengths, aspirations and priorities of a potential partner.

Ask what the organization is proud of, what it hopes to achieve and what a successful partnership would make possible. Listen for a shared affirmative topic before proposing a solution. This does not mean overlooking real problems or selling services that are not needed.

A worthwhile relationship is one in which both parties can name the value they hope to create and the commitments required to create it. Sustainable growth begins with honest discovery, mutual fit and reliable follow-through.

Closing reflection

What Makes People Want to Stay?

If you asked your employees what makes your organization worth staying for, what would they say? What might they say if they knew their answers would be heard without retaliation?

This week, ask one person what helps them thrive and what would make their experience of work better. Listen without defending. Agree on one action and a time to revisit it.
