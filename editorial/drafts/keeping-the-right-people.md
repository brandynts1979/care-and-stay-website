# Keeping the Right People: Retention and a Healthy Culture

A company can celebrate lower turnover and still have a culture in trouble. Employees may stay because they are valued and growing. They may also stay because they feel trapped, because the job market is uncertain, or because they have stopped expecting better.

The number of departures is worth measuring, but it is not the whole story.

For leaders hoping to build a Stay Culture, the more useful ambition is not simply to keep people. It is to create the conditions where the *right people* can do meaningful work and want to keep doing it.

## Who are the right people?

The phrase needs care. It must not become shorthand for people who agree with management, fit one personality type, or never raise concerns.

The right people are not necessarily the easiest people to manage. They may be the ones who notice what could improve, who advocate for customers or patients, and who challenge an ineffective process. A healthy culture makes room for constructive dissent.

A more useful definition includes shared commitments: acting with integrity, contributing to the mission, treating colleagues with respect, learning, and being willing to engage in the work of improvement. It also means asking whether the organization is offering fair expectations, sufficient support, and opportunities to succeed.

Fit is a two-way responsibility.

## Don't confuse comfort with health

In an unhealthy workplace, conflict can become chronic or personal. But the absence of disagreement is not proof of health. Teams need to talk about priorities, performance, and behavior—even when those conversations are difficult.

A leader might ask, *Where have we mistaken silence for agreement?* The answer may reveal opportunities to strengthen trust and accountability together.

Likewise, a good culture is not a culture without standards. Fair standards and thoughtful coaching protect colleagues who consistently carry the work, while also giving struggling employees a genuine opportunity to improve.

## Ask what is worth preserving

An appreciative approach offers a starting point that is both practical and hopeful: identify the conditions under which the team has been at its best.

Invite people to describe a time when they felt proud of their work. What did the team do? What did the leader do? Which resources, relationships, or practices made the difference?

Then ask what stands in the way of repeating those moments. What should be strengthened? What should change? What can no longer be accepted?

This is not a substitute for discussing painful realities. It is a way to understand both what people value and what prevents them from experiencing more of it.

## When staying isn't the right outcome

Sometimes a role changes. Sometimes an individual's priorities change. Sometimes expectations remain unmet despite real support and clear feedback. A healthy organization can handle these moments with honesty, dignity, and care.

The same values that guide retention should guide transitions. How leaders treat someone who is leaving sends a message to everyone who remains.

A Stay Culture can therefore welcome healthy movement while taking avoidable, harmful turnover seriously.

## Three questions for leaders

If you're reviewing retention today, add three questions alongside the turnover rate:

1. **Who is thriving, and why?** Look for conditions you can reproduce rather than assuming success happened by accident.
2. **Who might be quietly disengaging?** Make it safe to speak before a resignation becomes the first honest conversation.
3. **What are we asking people to tolerate?** Examine whether schedules, unclear decisions, inconsistent accountability, or unnecessary friction are undermining the culture you say you value.

The goal is not to keep everyone forever. The goal is to build a workplace where contribution, growth, belonging, and integrity give people genuine reasons to stay.

*Reflection:* If your most thoughtful employee gave you an unfiltered account of what it feels like to work here, what would you hope they would say—and what might they say instead?
