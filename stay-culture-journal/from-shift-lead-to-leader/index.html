From Shift Lead to Leader: The Promotion Nobody Prepares You For

Care & Stay editorial draft · Byline and publication date pending approval

Yesterday, you were the person everyone could count on to keep the shift moving. Today, you're responsible for helping everyone else succeed. The skills that earned you the promotion matter, but they aren't the whole job anymore.

The promotion changes the question

A strong shift lead often earns trust by being quick, accurate, and willing to jump in wherever needed. Those qualities remain valuable after a promotion. Yet managing a restaurant team adds different questions: Can I set expectations clearly? Can I coach someone without humiliating them? Can I address a recurring problem without making the whole team defensive?

A new manager who continues to solve every problem personally may look indispensable while the team becomes increasingly dependent. Leadership asks not only what you can accomplish, but what you help other people become capable of accomplishing.

Coach before you correct

Imagine an employee who struggles during a busy handoff. An automatic reaction might be to say, “You need to speed up.” A coach-like response is more curious and more specific: “Where did the handoff become difficult? What would help you be ready for the next rush?”

Curiosity is not the absence of accountability. The manager can still clarify the standard, demonstrate a better approach, and follow up. Coaching gives a person both an expectation and a genuine opportunity to meet it.

Your former peers are watching

Becoming the supervisor of people who were recently your peers can be awkward. Trying to remain everyone's friend can make standards inconsistent. Adopting an unnecessarily harsh voice can erode the trust that made the promotion possible.

The alternative is steady credibility: be fair, explain decisions, recognize contributions, and talk privately when performance needs attention. Avoid using authority as proof of status. Use it to create conditions in which the team can succeed.

Make appreciation specific

A rushed “good job” may be pleasant, but it tells an employee little about what to repeat. More useful recognition describes a contribution: “You noticed the handoff was falling behind, stepped in without being asked, and helped the next team start ready.”

That kind of appreciation strengthens shared understanding of what good work looks like. It is particularly meaningful when it goes beyond praising only the most visible or outgoing employees.

A ten-minute leadership habit

Before a shift, identify one person you can develop. During the shift, notice a specific strength and a real obstacle. After the rush, take a few minutes to ask what went well, what made the job difficult, and what the employee would try next time.

Then follow up. New managers do not become effective through a single inspirational speech. They grow through repeated conversations, clear expectations, and the courage to learn alongside their teams.

For your reflection

Who on your team is becoming more capable because of the way you lead—and what evidence do you have?
