Leading people well

The conversation before the resignation

Care & Stay · Editorial draft for review · November 12, 2026

A resignation can invite a conversation that would have been useful months earlier. What was difficult? What was missing? What could we have done differently?

There is value in asking those questions. There is also value in asking while someone is still here, still contributing, and still deciding what this workplace means to them.

Begin with a real moment

An appreciative conversation does not require pretending everything is going well. It begins by looking closely at an experience worth understanding.

When did work feel meaningful to you recently—and what made that possible?

The answer might involve a manager who made time to listen, a teammate who stepped in, or an opportunity to learn something new. Follow the story. Ask what happened, who helped, and which conditions made the experience possible.

Then make room for what is harder: “What gets in the way of having more days like that?” Strengths and difficulties can belong in the same conversation.

Listen without building a defense

When someone describes a frustrating experience, a leader may feel an immediate urge to explain. There may be context the person does not have. There may also be something the leader has not understood.

Before offering an explanation, try reflecting what you heard: “It sounds like the uncertainty about the schedule makes it difficult to plan your life outside work. Have I understood that?”

This does not promise that every request can be granted. It makes understanding part of the work.

Choose one action you can keep

Ask, “What is one change that would make a meaningful difference?” Discuss what is possible, who will take responsibility, and when you will return to the conversation. If a request cannot be met, explain the constraint plainly and explore what can be done.

An invitation to speak carries a responsibility to respond. Even a modest action can give the conversation substance when the commitment is clear and the follow-through is visible.

Try it this week

  1. Invite one team member to a short conversation at a time that works for them.
  2. Ask about a recent meaningful moment and what helped create it.
  3. Ask what is making work harder than it needs to be.
  4. Agree on one realistic next step and a date to check back.

A question for your own reflection

What have people already told me that I have not yet acted on or responded to?

A Stay Culture takes shape through repeated experiences of being heard, respected, and able to contribute. A thoughtful conversation is one place to begin.
